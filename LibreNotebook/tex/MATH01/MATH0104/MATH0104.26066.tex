\section{Additional Topics}
Involution, monotonicity


Some interesting problems gives bound for the function for us to
use bounding argument.

\begin{important}
The key to these kind of problems is to perform certain operations on
the function and show that it contradicts with the given criteria.
\end{important}

Here is an interesting problem from the document
\textit{169 Functional Equations} by Moubinool the showcase the
bounding through a specific criteria given by the problem.

\begin{problem}{}{}
Find all functions $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that
$\left\{ \frac{f(x)}{x} \mid x \in \mathbb{R} \text{ and } x \neq 0
\right\}$ is a finite set and $f(x - 1 - f(x)) = f(x) - x - 1$ for all
real $x$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
For this problem, because the terms inside $f$ in the left-hand
side and the terms in the right-hand side are similar, let's try
and make them equal like the following.
\[
f(x - 1 - f(x)) = -(x - 1 - f(x)) - 2
\]
Let $\alpha = x - 1 - f(x)$ and we get $f(\alpha) = -\alpha - 2$.
But there is a problem here. The number of terms for $\frac{f(x)}{x}$
is bounded, but we have infinitely many $\frac{-\alpha - 2}{\alpha}$.
This tells us that $\alpha$ is finite for the condition to be
satisfied.

Further observing the problem, let's substitute $\alpha$ for $x$.
We obtain
\begin{align*}
    f(\alpha - 1 - f(\alpha))
    &= -(\alpha - 1 - f(\alpha)) - 2 \\
    f(\alpha - 1 + \alpha + 2) &= -\alpha - 1 - \alpha - 2
\end{align*}
and $f(2\alpha + 1) = -(2\alpha + 1) - 2$. This shows that
$2\alpha + 1$ is also an element of a finite set, say $A$,
that contains $\alpha$.

Continuing, we can try and substitute $2\alpha + 1$ for $x$.
We obtain $f(2\alpha + 1 - 1 + 2\alpha + 1 + 2) = -(2\alpha + 1) - 2
- 2\alpha - 1 - 1$ and $f(4\alpha + 3) = -(4\alpha + 3) - 2$.
This shows that $4\alpha + 3 \in A$. From the patterns, we could
hypothesize that $2^n \alpha + 2^n - 1 \in A$. To show this,
it suffices to show that if $2^{n-1} \alpha + 2^{n-1} - 1 \in A$,
then $2^n \alpha + 2^n - 1 \in A$. Consider the following equations
that we get for substituting $x = 2^{n-1} \alpha + 2^{n-1} - 1$.
\begin{align*}
    &\quad\ f \left(
        2^{n-1} \alpha + 2^{n-1} - 1 - 1
            + \left( 2^{n-1} \alpha + 2^{n-1} - 1) + 2 \right)
    \right) \\
    &= -\left( 2^{n-1} \alpha + 2^{n-1} - 1 \right) - 2
        - \left( 2^{n-1} \alpha + 2^{n-1} - 1 \right) - 1 \\
    &\qquad\quad
    f \left( 2^n \alpha + 2^n - 1 \right)
    = -\left( 2^n \alpha + 2^n - 1 \right) - 2
\end{align*}
Indeed by induction, $2^n \alpha + 2^n - 1 \in A$ for all positive
integers $n$. In other words, $\alpha = -1$ is the only possible
case and $\alpha = x - 1 - f(x) = -1$. Therefore, $f(x) = x$ is the
only solution.
\end{solution}

Some interesting problems don't give us the options for classic
discoveries.

\begin{important}
In such problems, it is very important to not underestimate the
condition $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.
\end{important}

Here is an example problem where $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ acts
as a breakthrough of the solution.

\begin{problem}{2014 KMO Problem 2 \medium}{2014 KMO Problem 2}
Find all functions $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that the
following equation holds for all $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$.
\vspace{-0.8\baselineskip}
\[
f(xf(x) + f(x)f(y) + y - 1) = f(xf(x) + xy) + y - 1
\]
\end{problem}
(\href{https://youtu.be/OXDSWs-f9NA}{Video Solution})
\begin{solution}
This problem is very interesting because just by looking at the
equation, it is evident that $f(x) = x$ works. However, it is quite
difficult to find anything else other than that, so proving the
uniqueness of the solution is the main part of this problem.

First, substitute $x = 0$ to the equation. Then, we can see that
the following equation holds.
\[
f(f(0)f(y) + y - 1) = f(0) + y - 1
\]
Now how is this supposed to be useful? At glance, it isn't. There are
limited information that we can learn from this equation. However,
if you look very closely at the equation, we can see that $f(0)$ is
defined since $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ and $y - 1$ can be
any real number. Meaning, $f$ is surjective!

From here, we are pretty much done. Because $f$ is surjective,
we can choose some $a$ such that $f(a) = 0$. Substituting $x = a$,
we obtain $f(y - 1) = f(ay) + y - 1$. Therefore, it is natural for
us to consider the case when $ay = y - 1$, which is
$y = \frac{1}{1 - a}$. However, we have to be careful here because
we are implying that $a \neq 1$.

Assume for the contradiction that $a = 1$ is unique value such that
$f(a) = 0$. Substituting $x = y = 1$, we obtain $f(0) = 0$ and
$a = 1$ is not unique if $f(1) = 0$. In other words, we can choose
some $a \neq 1$ such that $ay = y - 1$, which will give us $y - 1
= \frac{a}{1 - a} = 0$. Solving the equation, $a = 0$ and $f(0) = 0$.

Substituting $x = 0$ back to the equation, we get $f(y - 1) = y - 1$
and $f(x) = x$ is the unique answer.
\end{solution}

Unlike other problems, this one requires some effort to find that
$f(0) = 0$. In this problem $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ was an
important information for concluding that $f$ is surjective.
