\section{Isogonal Conjugate}

The isogonal conjugate of a point $P$ with respect to a triangle
is an interesting well-defined point that can be expanded to
different techniques and patterns. Although the name suggests
the definition of the point, here is the definition.

\begin{definition}{}{}
The \textbf{isogonal conjugate} of a point $P$ with respect to a
triangle $ABC$ is the intersection of the reflections of $AP, BP, CP$
with respect to the angle bisectors at $A$, $B$, and $C$.
\end{definition}

Note that the lines $AQ, BQ, CQ$ are the \textbf{isogonal lines} of
$AP, BP, CP$ respectively. The point $Q$ is the isogonal conjugate
of $P$ with respect to $\triangle{ABC}$. Now this point $Q$ is
well-defined. To show that the isogonal conjugate is always a
well-defined point, we can use many different methods, but here
are two of my favorite ones.

For the first proof, we will use Angle-Ceva Theorem. I honestly am
not sure the standard term for the theorem because it is not
widely known, but in Korea we call it as Angle-Ceva Theorem and
I also saw some English forms like the angle form of Ceva' Theorem
and trigonometric form of Ceva's Theorem.

\begin{important}
The theorem states that
the following equation holds for some point $P$ in triangle $ABC$.
\[
\frac{\sin\angle{ABP}}{\sin\angle{PBC}} \cdot
    \frac{\sin\angle{CAP}}{\sin\angle{PAB}} \cdot
    \frac{\sin\angle{BCP}}{\sin\angle{PCA}}
= 1
\]
\end{important}

Note that by construction, the following equation holds.
\begin{align*}
    &\quad\ \frac{\sin\angle{CBQ}}{\sin\angle{QBA}} \cdot
        \frac{\sin\angle{ACQ}}{\sin\angle{QCB}} \cdot
        \frac{\sin\angle{BAQ}}{\sin\angle{QAC}} \\
    &= \frac{\sin\angle{ABP}}{\sin\angle{PBC}} \cdot
        \frac{\sin\angle{BCP}}{\sin\angle{PCA}} \cdot
        \frac{\sin\angle{CAP}}{\sin\angle{PAB}}
    = 1
\end{align*}
Therefore by reverse Angle-Ceva, $Q$ is well-defined.

Because we do not yet know if there exists point $Q$,
let $l_A, l_B, l_C$ be the respective isogonal lines. Notice that
by construction, because $AF = AP = AE$, $l_A$ is the perpendicular
bisector of $FE$. Analogously, it is self-evident that $l_B$ and
$l_C$ are perpendicular bisector of $FD$ and $ED$ respectively.
In other words, the point of intersection of $l_A, l_B, l_C$
is the center of the circumcircle of $\triangle{DEF}$ and the center
of such circle and the points $D, E, F$ always exist. Thus,
$Q$ is well-defined.

Points $H$ and $O$ are isogonal conjugates.
\end{important}

Before we wrap up this section, let's discuss about a special case
called symmedian.

\begin{definition}{}{}
The \textbf{symmedian} from a vertex of a triangle is the
isogonal line of the line that passes through the vertex and
the midpoint of its opposite side.
\end{definition}

Here, $M$ is the midpoint of side $BC$ and $AK$ is the symmedian
from $A$. With this definition, we can construct the following
theorem.

\begin{theorem}{}{Symmedian and Lengths}
Given a triangle $ABC$ with midpoint $M$ of side $BC$, $AK$ is the
symmedian if and only if $\frac{CK}{BK} = \frac{AC^2}{AB^2}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First, consider the following diagram where $D, E$ are points on $AB$
drawn perpendicularly from $M$ and $K$ and $F, G$ are point on $AC$
drawn perpendicularly from $M$ and $K$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
    \coordinate (A) at (6,5);
    \coordinate (B) at (0,0);
    \coordinate (C) at (8,0);
    \coordinate (D) at (2.360655737704918,1.9672131147540983);
    \coordinate (E) at (3.201820175399282,2.6681834794994015);
    \coordinate (F) at (7.448275862068965,1.3793103448275863);
    \coordinate (G) at (7.644869849422821,0.8878253764429483);
    \coordinate (K) at (5.42530640831545,0);
    \coordinate (M) at (4,0);

    \draw[thick] (A) -- (B) -- (C) -- cycle;
    \draw[thick] (A) -- (K);
    \draw[thick] (A) -- (M);
    \draw[thick, BrickRed, dotted] (M) -- (D);
    \draw[thick, BrickRed, dotted] (D) -- (F);

        node[above left] {\footnotesize $E$};
    \draw[fill=RoyalPurple] (F) circle (0.05)
        node[above right] {\footnotesize $F$};
    \draw[fill=RoyalPurple] (G) circle (0.05)
        node[right] {\footnotesize $G$};
    \draw[fill=black] (K) circle (0.05)
        node[below] {\footnotesize $K$};

        node[below] {\footnotesize $M$};

    \pic[draw, angle radius=8mm, angle eccentricity=1.4]
        {angle=B--A--M};
    \pic[draw, angle radius=8mm, angle eccentricity=1.4]
        {angle=K--A--C};

    \pic[draw, angle radius=1.5mm] {right angle=K--E--B};
    \pic[draw, angle radius=1.5mm] {right angle=M--D--B};
    \pic[draw, angle radius=1.5mm] {right angle=M--F--C};
    \pic[draw, angle radius=1.5mm] {right angle=K--G--C};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{figure}

Notice that because $\square{ADMF}$ and $\square{AEKG}$ are cyclic,
$\angle{DMF} = \angle{EKG}$. Moreover by construction, because
$AK$ is the symmedian from $A$, $\angle{DFM} = \angle{KEG}$.
Therefore, $\triangle{DMF} \sim \triangle{GKE}$ and $\frac{GK}{KE}
= \frac{DM}{MF}$.

Continuing, notice that because $\frac{1}{2} MD \cdot AB =
\frac{1}{2} MF \cdot AC$, $\frac{MD}{MG} = \frac{AC}{AB}$. In other
words, $\frac{GK}{KE} = \frac{AC}{AB}$ and $\frac{[\triangle{AKC}]}
{[\triangle{ABK}]} = \frac{AC^2}{AB^2}$. Moreover, because the
entire process is reversible, the theorem holds.
\end{proof}
