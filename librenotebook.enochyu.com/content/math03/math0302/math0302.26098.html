0.1 Curvature

Before we get into curvature, we need to discuss some important topics and notation that will be used throughout the notes. As always, let’s get started with definition.

Definition 0.1.1

Given a vector-valued function \(\mathbf {r}\) of a smooth curve \(C\), the unit tangent vector to \(C\) is defined as the following. \[ \mathbf {T}(t) = \frac {\mathbf {r}'(t)}{\| \mathbf {r}'(t) \|} \]

As the name suggests, \(\| \mathbf {T}(T) \| = \frac {\| \mathbf {r}'(t) \|}{\| \mathbf {r}'(t) \|} = 1\). Also, it is worth noting that the unit tangent vector at a point of all smooth parametrizations of a curve are the same because the tangent vectors are normalized. Continuing, we can define another term.

Definition 0.1.2

Given a smooth parametrization \(\mathbf {r}\) of a curve and the unit tangent vector \(\mathbf {T}(t)\), the unit normal vector to the curve at \(t\) is defined as the following. \[ \mathbf {N}(t) = \frac {\mathbf {T}'(t)}{\| \mathbf {T}'(t) \|} \]

Notice that because \(\mathbf {T}'(t) \equiv 0\) if a curve is a straight line, \(\mathbf {N}(t)\) is not defined. Moreover, notice that because \(\mathbf {T}(t) \cdot \mathbf {T}(t) = 1\) and \(2\mathbf {T}'(t) \cdot \mathbf {T}(t) = 0\), \(\mathbf {N}(t)\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf {T}(t)\) as the name suggests! However, there are different ways for \(\mathbf {N}\) to be orthogonal to \(\mathbf {T}\) geometrically. For instance, in 2-space, we have two directions and we have infinitely many possibilities in 3-space. To clarify the direction in which \(\mathbf {N}\) is orthogonal in 2-space, consider the following theorem.

Theorem 0.1.3

Given a smooth curve \(C\) in 2-space, the unit normal vector at a point on the curve is towards the concave side.

Proof.

Notice that to show that \(\mathbf {N}(t)\) point towards the concave side of the curve, it suffices to show that the curve bends towards the direction of \(\mathbf {T}'(t)\). Let \(\theta (t)\) be the angle of \(\mathbf {T}\) from the positive \(x\)-axis. Notice that because \(\mathbf {T}\) is a unit vector, \(\mathbf {T}\) can be written as \(\mathbf {T} = (\cos \theta (t), \sin \theta (t))\).

Continuing, \(\mathbf {T}'(t) = \theta '(t)(-\sin \theta (t), \cos \theta (t))\), which can be obtained by rotating \(\mathbf {T}\) by \(90^\circ \) and multiplying by \(\theta '(t)\). Note that if \(\theta '(t) > 0\), then it is pointing towards the left, or towards the concave side, of the curve. Similarly, if \(\theta '(t)\), then \(\mathbf {T}'(t)\) is pointing towards the left, or towards the concave side, of the curve. Because the normal vector is not defined when \(\theta '(t) = 0\), the theorem holds.

Before we get into curvature, here is one last definition.

Definition 0.1.4

Given a smooth curve in 3-space, the binormal vector to the curve at \(t\) is defined as \(\mathbf {B}(t) = \mathbf {T}(t) \times \mathbf {N}(t)\).

From the definition, we can infer that \(\mathbf {B}\) is orthogonal to both unit tangent vector and unit normal vector, and is also a unit vector. The coordinate system using \(\mathbf {N}\), \(\mathbf {T}\), and \(\mathbf {B}\) is known as Frenet frame or TNB-frame.

One more thing that we can notice from the definition is that \(\mathbf {B}(t)\) can be written as the following at \(t\) and \(s\) if the parametrization is \(\mathbf {r}(s)\). \begin{align*} \mathbf {B}(t) &= \frac {\mathbf {r}'(t) \times \mathbf {r}''(t)} {\| \mathbf {r}'(t) \times \mathbf {r}''(t) \|} \\ \mathbf {B}(s) &= \frac {\mathbf {r}'(s) \times \mathbf {r}''(s)} {\| \mathbf {r}'(s) \times \mathbf {r}''(s) \|} = \frac {\mathbf {r}'(s) \times \mathbf {r}''(s)} {\| \mathbf {r}''(s) \|} \end{align*}

Because the derivation is fairly straightforward with definition and \(\mathbf {r}' \times \mathbf {r}' = 0\), let’s skip the proof. Now let’s discuss about curvature!

Just for the sake of intuition, consider a circle and a square root function. From those two, the circle has larger curvature because it curves more, and we can represent that by the rate of changes in the direction of the tangent vector. Here is more rigorous definition.

Definition 0.1.5

Given an arc length parametrization of a curve, the curvature of the curve at \(s\) is defined as \(\kappa (s) = \| \mathbf {T}'(s) \|\).

In other words, curvature is the rate of change of the tangent vector with respect to its arc length. Notice that \(\kappa (s)\) is a function in terms of \(s\).

If the parametrization is not arc-length, then we write it as \(\kappa (t = t_0)\) for clarity and the following equation holds. \[ \kappa (t = t_0) = \frac {\| \mathbf {T_1}' (t_0) \|}{\| \mathbf {r_1}' (t_0) \|} \]

To see why this is true, consider the arc-length parametrization \(\mathbf {r_2}(s) = \mathbf {r_1}(g(s))\). Similarly, let \(\mathbf {T_1}\) and \(\mathbf {T_2}\) be the respective unit tangent vectors. Then, the following equations hold. \begin{align*} \kappa (t = t_0) &= \kappa (s = s_0) = \| \mathbf {T_2}'(s_0) \| = \| \mathbf {T_1}'(g(s_0)) \cdot g'(s_0) \| \\ &= \frac {\| \mathbf {T_1}'(g(s_0)) \cdot g'(s_0) \|} {\| \mathbf {r_2}'(s_0) \|} = \frac {\mathbf {T_1}'(g(s_0))}{\mathbf {r_1}'(g(s_0))} \end{align*}

Therefore, the equation holds.

Continuing, here is another interesting theorem. This is quite important because many times curvature is defined and used with this theorem, or formula, below.

Theorem 0.1.6

Let \(\mathbf {r}(t)\) be a smooth parametrization of a curve in 3-space. If \(\mathbf {r}''(t_0)\) is defined, then the following equation holds. \[ \kappa (t = t_0) = \frac { \| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \times \mathbf {r}''(t_0) \| } {\| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \|^3} \]

Proof.

Define \(\mathbf {r_1}(s) = \mathbf {r}(g(s))\) where \(g\) is an arc length change. Then, it suffices to show that \(\| \mathbf {r_1}''(s_0) \| = \frac { \| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \times \mathbf {r}''(t_0) \| } {\| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \|^3}\).

Notice that by construction, the following equations hold. \begin{align*} \mathbf {r_1}'(s) &= \mathbf {r}'(g(s)) g'(s) \\ \mathbf {r_1}''(s) &= \mathbf {r}''(g(s)) g'(s)^2 + \mathbf {r}'(g(s)) g''(s) \end{align*}

Therefore, the following equations hold. \begin{align*} \frac { \| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \times \mathbf {r}''(t_0) \| } {\| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \|^3} &= \frac { \| \mathbf {r}'(g(s_0)) \times \mathbf {r}''(g(s_0)) \| } {\| \mathbf {r}'(g(s_0)) \|^3} \\ &= \frac { \| \mathbf {r}'(g(s_0)) \times \mathbf {r}''(g(s_0)) \| | g'(s_0) |^3 }{\| \mathbf {r}'(g(s_0)) g'(s_0) \|^3} \\ &= \| \mathbf {r_1}'(s_0) \times \mathbf {r_1}''(s_0) \| \\ &= \| \mathbf {r_1}''(s_0) \| \end{align*}

Therefore, the equation holds.

Notice that if \(\mathbf {r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), 0 \rangle \), then this theorem also holds for 2-space. Let’s take a look at an example for computing curvature at a points of a curve.

Exercise 0.1.7

Find the curvature of the helix \(\mathbf {r}(t) = \langle a \cos t, a \sin t, bt \rangle \) at \(t\) given that \(a > 0\).

Solution.

First, recall that \(\kappa (t_0) = \frac { \| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \times \mathbf {r}''(t_0) \| }{\| \mathbf {r}'(t_0) \|^3}\). Moreover, the following equations hold. \begin{align*} \mathbf {r}'(t) &= \langle -a \sin t, a \cos t, b \rangle \\ \mathbf {r}''(t) &= \langle -a \cos t, -a \sin t, b \rangle \\ \mathbf {r}'(t) \times \mathbf {r}''(t) &= \langle ab \sin t, - ab \cos t, a^2 \rangle \end{align*}

Therefore, \(\| \mathbf {r}'(t) \times \mathbf {r}''(t) \| = a \sqrt {a^2 + b^2}\) and \(\| \mathbf {r}'(t) \| = \sqrt {a^2 + b^2}\). In other words, \(\kappa (t_0) = \frac {a}{a^2 + b^2}\).

In this case, we could see that the curvature is constant because it is a circular helix. The similar idea apply for 2-space.

Exercise 0.1.8

Given a circle \(\mathbf {r}(t) = \langle a \cos t, a \sin t \rangle \) with \(a > 0\), find the curvature at each point on the circle.

Solution.

First, notice that \begin{align*} \mathbf {r}'(t) &= \langle -a \sin t, a \cos t, 0 \rangle \\ \mathbf {r}''(t) &= \langle -a \cos t, -a \sin t, 0 \rangle \end{align*}

holds. Therefore, \(\kappa (t) = \frac {a^2}{(a^2 \sin ^2 t + a^2 \cos ^2 t)^\frac {3}{2}} = \frac {1}{a}\).

Again, the curvature is constant! Another interesting property of circle is that its curvature at each point is the reciprocal of its radius. Continuing with this observation, we can establish the following definitions.

Definition 0.1.9

Given a point \(P\) on a curve \(C\) with curvature \(\kappa _0\), there exists a unique circle \(\omega \) that is tangent to the line that is tangent to \(C\) at \(P\) and is on the concave side of \(C\). Such circle is known as osculating circle to \(C\) at \(P\) where the radius \(\frac {1}{\kappa _0}\) and its center are known as the radius of curvature and the center of curvature of \(C\) at \(P\).

From the definition and construction, we can notice that the osculating circle can be used to best approximate the curve at \(P\).

Another interesting property that we can notice from curvature for an arc-length parametrization in 2-space is its relationship with the angle that the unit tangent vector makes with the positive \(x\)-axis. Notice that \(\mathbf {T}(\theta ) = \langle \cos \theta , \sin \theta \rangle \). More \(\mathbf {T}(s) = \mathbf {T}(\theta (s)) = \langle \cos \theta (s), \sin \theta (s) \rangle \). Because \(\| \frac {d\mathbf {T}} {d\theta } = 1\), \(\kappa (s) = \| \frac {d\mathbf {T}}{ds} \| = \| \frac {d\mathbf {T}}{d\theta } \cdot \frac {d\theta }{ds} \| = | \frac {d\theta }{ds} |\).

Therefore, \(\kappa (s) = | \frac {d\theta }{ds} |\) if \(\theta \) is the angle that the unit tangent vector makes with the positive \(x\)-axis given an arc-length parameterization of a curve.

Before we wrap up this note, here are some interesting connections we can make with curvature and physics.

First, notice that \(\mathbf {v}(t) = \frac {ds}{dt} \mathbf {T}(t)\) given an arc-length parameterization. Therefore, the following equations hold. \begin{align*} \mathbf {a}(t) &= \frac {d^2s}{dt^2} \mathbf {T}(t) + \frac {ds}{dt} \mathbf {T}'(t) = \frac {d^2s}{dt^2} \mathbf {T}(t) + \| \mathbf {r}'(t) \| \mathbf {T}'(t) \\ &= \frac {d^2s}{dt^2} \mathbf {T}(t) + \| \mathbf {r}'(t) \| \mathbf {T}'(t) = \frac {d^2s}{dt^2} \mathbf {T}(t) + \frac { \| \mathbf {T}'(t) \| }{ \| \mathbf {r}'(t) \| } \cdot \| \mathbf {r}'(t) \|^2 \cdot \frac {\mathbf {T}'(t)}{ \| \mathbf {T}'(t) \| } \\ &= \frac {d^2s}{dt^2} \mathbf {T}(t) + \kappa (t) \| \mathbf {r}'(t) \|^2 \mathbf {N}(t) \end{align*}

Therefore, we can conclude the following.

Given an arc-length parametrization of position of a particle, its acceleration at \(t\) can be represented as the following. \[ \mathbf {a}(t) = \frac {d^2s}{dt^2} \mathbf {T}(t) + \kappa (t) \| \frac {ds}{dt} \|^2 \mathbf {N}(t) \]

We can also notice that this equation is consistent with centripetal force and acceleration in linear path! Continuing from this equation, we can establish the following definition.

Definition 0.1.10

Let \(a_T(t) = \frac {d^2s}{dt^2}\) and \(a_N(t) = \kappa (t) \| \frac {ds}{dt} \|^2\). Then \(\mathbf {a}(t) = a_T \mathbf {T} + a_N \mathbf {N}\). The terms \(a_T\) and \(a_N\) are known as the tangential component of acceleration and the normal component of acceleration. Moreover, \(a_T \mathbf {T}\) and \(a_N \mathbf {N}\) are known as the tangential vector component of acceleration and the normal vector component of acceleration respectively.

Continuing from this notation and definition, we can notice the following.

Given a smooth parametrization of the position of a particle, the following equations hold. \begin{align*} a_T &= \frac {\mathbf {v} \cdot \mathbf {a}}{\| \mathbf {v} \|} \\ a_N &= \frac {\mathbf {v} \times \mathbf {a}}{\| \mathbf {v} \|} \\ \kappa &= \frac {\mathbf {v} \times \mathbf {a}}{\| \mathbf {v} \|^3} \\ \| \mathbf {a} \|^2 &= a_T^2 + a_N^2 \end{align*}

The derivation of these equations are fairly straightforward, so let’s skip the proof. Finally, consider the following equations for projectile motion. \begin{align*} \mathbf {a} &= -g \mathbf {j} \\ \mathbf {v} &= -gt \mathbf {j} + \mathbf {v_0} \\ \mathbf {r} &= \left ( -\frac {gt^2}{2} + \mathbf {s_0} \right ) \mathbf {j} + t\mathbf {v_0} \end{align*}

Substituting \(\mathbf {v_0} = \langle x, y \rangle \), we can see that the following equation holds. \[ y = \left ( -\frac {g}{2a^2} \right ) x^2 + \frac {b}{a} x + \mathbf {s_0} \] Indeed projectile motions are parabolas, and this is it for this note!
